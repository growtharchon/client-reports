The fairest reading of the project is:

  • The client approved and continued to refine the initial design framework; the August 17 document also says the About and Policies pages looked great apart from specific photo/marquee changes, and that two other pages looked and worked great.
  • The July 30 and August 17 documents were processed through multiple revision passes and the requested changes were expected to be complete.
  • The August 19 audit nevertheless found several items from those completed revision passes still appearing on the public site. This is best described as a live-site QA/deployment discrepancy that needs reconciliation—not as feedback that was never reviewed or work that was never attempted.
  • Technical SEO foundations, keyword/competitor research, a content plan, Arketta booking, and Google Tag Manager are present. Planned articles and location pages are not yet published, and reporting access/results still need to be made visible to the client.
  • A redesign is possible, but it should be handled as a structured new art-direction decision with written scope, rather than another round of isolated Word-document edits.

4. Logo clarification

There is no technical limitation preventing the supplied logo from being made larger. The website team can scale the complete logo lockup proportionally to the size needed for a desktop or mobile placement.

The relevant constraint is standard brand practice: designers normally use the logo supplied by the client without stretching it, redrawing it, changing its aspect ratio, rearranging its elements, or enlarging one part independently from another. Those changes would modify the brand asset rather than simply size it for the website.

The correct implementation approach is to:

  1. Use the logo files supplied by the client as complete, unaltered brand assets.
  2. Scale the chosen lockup proportionally and show the client the resulting desktop and mobile placements.
  3. If the client wants a different relationship between the icon, wordmark, or tagline, use an alternate lockup formally supplied or approved by the logo designer.
  4. Do not stretch, crop, re-typeset, or independently resize parts of the logo in the website implementation.

7. Recommended recovery plan

Step 1 — Reconcile the deployed site with both revision documents

Because the changes were processed through multiple passes and were expected to be complete, first verify that the correct revision build is deployed. Then complete the five visible deletions listed in Section 3 and run a page-by-page QA comparison against both documents. Confirm the preferred overall logo size and testimonial set. Send the client a short reconciled checklist rather than asking her to restate the instructions.

Step 2 — Hold a focused brand-reset call

Use the call to define what “distinctive” means in observable terms. Ask for three sites or visual references she loves, three she dislikes, and a short list of brand traits. Discuss photography, typography, page rhythm, layout, and tone—not only individual sections.

Step 3 — Present two homepage art directions

Offer two genuinely different homepage concepts using the real logo, real photography, and approved copy. Present desktop and mobile hero/header treatments together. Approve one direction in writing before extending it across inner pages.

Step 4 — Separate corrections from redesign scope

The current site is based on the approved initial framework and repeated work against two detailed revision documents. Reconciling the deployed site with those instructions is completion QA. A wholesale visual reset is a new art-direction phase. Before redesign work begins, document what is included, the number of concept/revision rounds, schedule, responsibilities, and any fee or contract adjustment.

Step 5 — Make SEO visible

Provide a one-page baseline and recurring scorecard showing:

  • Technical: indexed pages, crawl issues, Core Web Vitals, uptime.
  • Visibility: impressions, clicks, average position, priority local queries.
  • Engagement: users, source/medium, landing pages, phone/email/directions clicks.
  • Conversion: booking starts and completed bookings where Arketta tracking permits.
  • Delivery: content planned, drafted, approved, published, and indexed.

8. Proposed phone-call agenda

  1. Listen first: what feels generic, what feels too close to Core Sport, and what she expected to feel but does not.
  2. Confirm live-site reconciliation: the processed-but-not-reflected deletions, proportional size of the supplied logo lockup, photos, and testimonials.
  3. Review the design record: acknowledge the approved initial framework without using approval as a reason to dismiss current concerns.
  4. Choose a path: focused refinement of the current direction or a structured visual redesign.
  5. Clarify SEO: show completed foundations and planning, what remains in setup, the publication schedule, and the client’s reporting access.
  6. End in writing: agreed scope, owners, dates, review points, and definition of final approval.
